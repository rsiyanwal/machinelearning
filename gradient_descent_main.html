≈ means approximately equal. A parameter may carry a unit such as minutes or degrees. A derivative’s unit is “objective units per parameter unit.”

A function is an input-output rule

Think of a function as a machine. Put in one value, follow the rule, and receive one output. Here the rule is g(x)=(x+2)².

g
The name of the rule.
x
The input supplied to the rule.
g(x)
The output produced for that input.
Notation: (x+2)² is an expression—a calculation. g(x)=(x+2)² is an equation stating that the function’s output equals that calculation. The symbol g names the whole rule; g(x) is the output for a particular input.

Horizontal movement changes the input. Vertical position shows the output. The lowest output is at x=−2.

From a real goal to a numerical score

Suppose bread bakes best near 200°C. Temperature is the choice we control. “Bake well” is too vague for an algorithm, so we assign a numerical penalty to temperatures away from 200.

score = J(T) = (T−200)² / 400

What each part means

  1. T−200 measures signed distance from the desired temperature.
  2. Squaring makes both too-cold and too-hot choices receive a positive penalty.
  3. Dividing by 400 changes the numerical scale, not the best temperature.
T
The controllable choice. We will soon call it a parameter.
J(T)
The score assigned to that choice. We will call it an objective.
Goal
Find a temperature that makes the score small while respecting real safety constraints.
Too coolJ(160)=4
DesiredJ(200)=0
Too hotJ(240)=4
An objective is a designed model of a goal. Real baking also depends on time, ingredients, oven accuracy, food safety, and preference. An optimizer follows the score we provide; it does not understand omitted concerns.

Imagine the real goal is:

“Make a good cup of tea.”

That goal involves many things:

  • Temperature
  • Amount of tea leaves
  • Brewing time
  • Sugar
  • Milk
  • Taste preference
  • Health restrictions

But suppose we give the optimizer only this objective:

J(T) = (T−65)²

where:

  • T is the tea temperature in °C.
  • 65°C is the desired temperature.
  • J(T) is the penalty score.
  • A smaller score is considered better.

The optimizer will correctly choose T=65, because that makes J(T)=0.

However, it does not know whether:

  • Tea leaves were added.
  • The tea brewed for long enough.
  • Ten spoons of sugar were added.
  • The milk was spoiled.
  • The person prefers hotter tea.
  • The person cannot consume sugar.

Those concerns were not included in its score.

Therefore:

“An optimizer follows the score we provide” means it searches for values that make the specified objective smaller.
“It does not understand omitted concerns” means it will not automatically consider anything that was left out of the objective or constraints.

It is like telling someone:

“Your only job is to serve the tea at exactly 65°C.”

They could serve tasteless, overly sweet tea at exactly 65°C and technically complete the assigned task.

A better objective might include more concerns:

J(T,s,b) = (T−65)² + 4(s−2)² + 2(b−4)²

where:

  • T is temperature.
  • s is the number of sugar teaspoons.
  • b is brewing time in minutes.
  • 65, 2, and 4 are the preferred values.
  • The numbers 4 and 2 determine how strongly sugar and brewing mistakes are penalized.

Even this improved objective remains an incomplete model of “good tea.” It still does not understand spoiled milk, allergies, cleanliness, cost, or personal preference unless those concerns are explicitly represented.

In short:

The optimizer solves the mathematical task we specify—not the complete real-world task we intended.

The idea we will name next: nearby height change tells us local steepness. Choosing the higher direction repeatedly is gradient ascent; choosing the lower direction repeatedly is gradient descent. This works with local information, so the starting position and landscape still matter.

Map the hill experience to the update equation

For the equation

xₖ₊₁ = xₖ − αf′(xₖ)
  • xₖ is your current position on the hill.
  • xₖ₊₁ is your next position after moving.
  • f′(xₖ) is the local slope at your current position. Its sign indicates the uphill direction, and its magnitude indicates steepness.
  • α is the learning rate. It controls how cautiously you respond to the slope.
  • αf′(xₖ) is the signed correction, so the actual movement depends on both the learning rate and the slope.

The distance used to probe is not the learning rate. Write the small distance reached by your feet as Δx. The two probes can estimate the derivative:

f′(xₖ) ≈ [f(xₖ+Δx) − f(xₖ−Δx)] / (2Δx)
Δx
The small horizontal probing distance to each side.
f(xₖ−Δx)
The height felt by the left foot.
f(xₖ+Δx)
The height felt by the right foot.
≈
“Approximately equal”; smaller probes usually give a more local estimate on a smooth surface.

Going to the top: gradient ascent

xₖ₊₁ = xₖ + αf′(xₖ)

You move in the derivative’s direction because the derivative points toward local increase.

Going to the bottom: gradient descent

xₖ₊₁ = xₖ − αf′(xₖ)

You move opposite the derivative because the opposite direction produces local decrease.

Numerical example

Suppose xₖ=0.2, f′(xₖ)=−2.88, and α=0.1.

Going uphill:

xₖ₊₁ = 0.2 + 0.1(−2.88) = −0.088

The negative derivative says uphill is toward the left, so the new position is smaller.

Going downhill:

xₖ₊₁ = 0.2 − 0.1(−2.88) = 0.488

Downhill is opposite the derivative, so the new position moves toward the right.

xₖ is where you are now. Probe by Δx to estimate the slope. Use +αf′(xₖ) to climb or −αf′(xₖ) to descend. The result xₖ₊₁ is where you stand next.

Discover local direction

Slope tells us what happens nearby

We now need a way to ask whether a small move to the right makes a score rise or fall. That local direction is what the derivative supplies.

Start with a ramp

Slope compares vertical change with horizontal change. A staircase calls these changes rise and run.

slope = rise / run

A curve needs a local slope

A curve does not have one slope everywhere. We estimate the slope near x=3 using two nearby points, then move the second point closer.

We never divide by zero. The derivative is the limit of these slopes as Δx approaches zero.

Derivative means local sensitivity

The derivative tells how quickly a function’s output changes when its input changes by a tiny amount. Its sign gives direction; its magnitude gives steepness.

Positive derivativeMoving right makes the output rise nearby.
Negative derivativeMoving right makes the output fall nearby.
Zero derivativeThe graph is locally flat; it may be a minimum, maximum, or saddle-like flat point.

d/dx
“Differentiate with respect to x.”
n
The exponent, or power, on x.

A positive derivative means the curve rises as we move right. A negative derivative means it falls as we move right. A zero derivative marks a flat point.

3
Give the symbols a meaning

What are we changing, and why?

Before using an update rule, treat optimization as a familiar activity: adjust something you control until a score describing the outcome becomes as good as possible.

1. Parameter: the adjustable knob

A parameter is a value we are allowed to change. It might be a departure time, oven temperature, launch angle, beam thickness, or a model weight.

In our first mathematics problem there is only one parameter, and we call it x.

2. Objective: the score

An objective is a numerical score that tells us how good or bad the current choice is. Gradient descent searches for parameter values that make this score smaller.

The objective must return a number so different choices can be compared.

3. Optimization: improve the choice

Optimization means finding parameter values that give the best objective value. An update is one attempted improvement to those values.

If a real goal is to maximize a reward R, we can instead minimize −R.

An objective is a model of the goal—not the whole goal. A GPS may treat travel time as the objective, but a person may also care about tolls, safety, or scenery. Choosing the right objective is part of solving the problem.

Return to our mathematical practice problem

Here x is a deliberately unit-free adjustable knob. It is our parameter—the current choice we are testing. The function below is the objective that scores each choice:

objective score = f(x) = x² + 4x

For example, choosing x=3 gives f(3)=3²+4·3=21. Choosing x=2 gives f(2)=2²+4·2=12. Because 12<21, the second choice is better when our aim is to minimize the objective.

Lab 1 · Choose a departure time

1 / 6

The problem

A week of measurements suggests that the commute is fastest near 7:15 a.m. Let x be the number of minutes after 7:00 a.m. that we depart.

x
Departure time in minutes after 7:00.
J(x)
Predicted travel time in minutes; this is the objective to minimize.
J(x) = 20 + (x−15)²/20

The baseline trip takes 20 minutes. The squared term adds a penalty as departure time moves away from 7:15.

Build the equation step by step

  1. Measure departure relative to the best observed time: x−15.
  2. Square it so early and late departures both receive a positive penalty: (x−15)².
  3. Divide by 20 to set the scale, then add the 20-minute baseline.
  4. Differentiate: J′(x)=(x−15)/10.
xₖ₊₁ = xₖ − 2J′(xₖ)

We start at x₀=50 (7:50 a.m.) and use α=2.

Derive the slope step by step

We differentiate with respect to x, because departure time is the value GD is allowed to change. The numbers 20 and 15 stay fixed.

1 · Rewrite the division. A fixed multiplier can be carried through a derivative.
J(x) = 20 + (1/20)(x−15)²
2 · Differentiate the sum. The derivative of a constant is zero.
J′(x) = 0 + (1/20) d/dx[(x−15)²]
3 · Use the chain rule. Let u=x−15. Then du/dx=1 and d(u²)/dx=2u·du/dx.
d/dx[(x−15)²] = 2(x−15)·1
4 · Simplify.
J′(x) = (1/20)·2(x−15) = (x−15)/10
5 · Evaluate the starting point. This is the slope GD uses for its first update.
J′(50) = (50−15)/10 = 35/10 = 3.5

Lab 2 · Tune an oven temperature

2 / 6

The problem

In a controlled classroom experiment, a texture test identifies 200°C as the target setting. Let T be oven temperature in degrees Celsius.

e(T)
Temperature error: T−200.
J(T)
Squared, scaled temperature-error loss.
e(T) = T−200
J(T) = e(T)²/400 = (T−200)²/400

Build the loss step by step

  1. Prediction or setting: the chosen temperature T.
  2. Target: 200°C, so the error is T−200.
  3. Square the error so hotter and colder settings are both penalized.
  4. Differentiate: J′(T)=(T−200)/200.
Tₖ₊₁ = Tₖ − 40J′(Tₖ)

We start at T₀=150°C and use α=40.

Derive the loss slope step by step

We differentiate with respect to T, the oven setting GD may change. The target 200 and scale 400 are fixed numbers.

1 · Rewrite the division.
J(T) = (1/400)(T−200)²
2 · Keep the fixed multiplier.
J′(T) = (1/400) d/dT[(T−200)²]
3 · Use the chain rule. Let u=T−200. Then du/dT=1.
d/dT[(T−200)²] = 2(T−200)·1
4 · Simplify.
J′(T) = [2(T−200)]/400 = (T−200)/200
5 · Evaluate the starting point. A negative slope says increasing T locally lowers the loss.
J′(150) = (150−200)/200 = −50/200 = −0.25

This equation teaches optimization only; actual safe baking settings depend on the recipe, equipment, food-safety guidance, and measured internal temperature.

The physics model

A ball is launched at speed v=25 m/s on level ground. Ignore air resistance. We want a horizontal range of 50 m and adjust the angle θ, measured in degrees.

R(θ) = (v²/g) sin(2θπ/180)
R(θ)
Predicted horizontal range in metres.
v
Launch speed; here v=25 m/s.
g
Gravitational acceleration, 9.8 m/s².
π
Pi, approximately 3.14159; π/180 converts degrees to radians.
sin, cos
The sine and cosine trigonometric functions. Sine appears in the range model; cosine appears when that model is differentiated.

Turn target miss into a loss

  1. Range error: R(θ)−50.
  2. Squared loss: J(θ)=½[R(θ)−50]².
  3. R′(θ)=(v²/g)cos(2θπ/180)(2π/180).
  4. Chain rule: J′(θ)=[R(θ)−50]R′(θ).
θₖ₊₁ = θₖ − 0.2J′(θₖ)

We start at θ₀=20° and use α=0.2.

Derive the range and loss slopes step by step

Here one derivative is nested inside another: angle changes range, and range changes the miss loss. We differentiate with respect to the angle θ in degrees; v, g, and the target 50 m stay fixed.

1 · Name the angle inside sine. The factor π/180 converts degrees to radians.
u(θ)=2θπ/180, so u′(θ)=2π/180
2 · Differentiate the range. The chain rule says d[sin(u)]/dθ=cos(u)u′.
R′(θ) = (v²/g) cos(2θπ/180)(2π/180)
3 · Name the miss. Let r(θ)=R(θ)−50; therefore r′(θ)=R′(θ).
J(θ) = (1/2)[r(θ)]²
4 · Differentiate the squared miss. The 2 from the power rule cancels the 1/2.
J′(θ) = (1/2)·2r(θ)r′(θ) = [R(θ)−50]R′(θ)
5 · Evaluate the starting point. Using R(20°)≈40.994 and R′(20°)≈1.705:
J′(20°) ≈ (40.994−50)(1.705) ≈ −15.36

The visualization searches only the lower-angle interval from 10° to 45°. The idealized projectile equation can also produce a second, higher-angle solution.

Lab 4 · Balance production costs

4 / 6

The problem

A small factory chooses production quantity q. Demand is near 100 units, but producing and storing inventory both cost money.

C(q) = 200 + 2q + 0.1(q−100)²
200
Fixed operating cost.
2q
Production cost of 2 cost-units per item.
0.1(q−100)²
Smooth shortage-or-surplus penalty around demand.

Build the objective step by step

  1. Add every cost that should influence the decision.
  2. Use a square so both underproduction and overproduction are penalized.
  3. No separate loss is required: total cost C(q) is already the objective.
  4. Differentiate: C′(q)=2+0.2(q−100).
qₖ₊₁ = qₖ − 2C′(qₖ)

We start at q₀=40 and use α=2.

Derive the cost slope step by step

We differentiate with respect to production quantity q. Each term tells how one part of total cost changes when the factory produces one more unit.

1 · Differentiate term by term. The derivative of a sum is the sum of its derivatives.
C′(q) = d(200)/dq + d(2q)/dq + d[0.1(q−100)²]/dq
2 · Fixed cost has zero slope. It does not change with q.
d(200)/dq = 0
3 · Differentiate linear cost. Since d(q)/dq=1:
d(2q)/dq = 2
4 · Differentiate the penalty. Use the fixed-multiplier and chain rules; d(q−100)/dq=1.
d[0.1(q−100)²]/dq = 0.1·2(q−100)·1 = 0.2(q−100)
5 · Combine and evaluate.
C′(q)=2+0.2(q−100);   C′(40)=2+0.2(−60)=−10

Lab 5 · Trade material against deflection

5 / 6

The teaching model

Let t>0 be a beam’s simplified thickness index. More thickness increases material cost, while too little thickness creates a rapidly increasing deflection penalty.

J(t) = 4t + 200/t²
4t
Material-cost term, increasing with thickness.
200/t²
Deflection penalty, decreasing as thickness grows.

Build the objective step by step

  1. Represent the expense of using more material with an increasing term.
  2. Represent poor stiffness at small thickness with a decreasing inverse-square term.
  3. Add the competing penalties into one objective.
  4. Differentiate: J′(t)=4−400/t³.
tₖ₊₁ = tₖ − 0.03J′(tₖ)

We start at t₀=2.5 and use α=0.03.

Derive the design-objective slope step by step

We differentiate with respect to thickness t. Because thickness must be positive, this model uses the domain t>0.

1 · Rewrite the fraction as a power. The identity 1/t²=t⁻² lets us use the power rule.
J(t) = 4t + 200t⁻²
2 · Differentiate term by term.
J′(t) = d(4t)/dt + d(200t⁻²)/dt
3 · Apply the power rule. For tⁿ, the derivative is ntⁿ⁻¹, even when n is negative.
J′(t) = 4 + 200(−2)t⁻³ = 4−400t⁻³
4 · Rewrite with a fraction.
J′(t) = 4−400/t³
5 · Evaluate the starting point.
J′(2.5)=4−400/(2.5)³=4−400/15.625=−21.6

This is not a structural-design equation. Real beam design must follow material models, load calculations, safety factors, constraints, and applicable engineering codes.

The hillside in the dark

Imagine trying to reach a village at the bottom of a hillside after dark. You cannot see the entire terrain. At your current position, you feel which nearby direction slopes downward most strongly, take a careful step, and inspect again.

This is the book’s most useful intuition: a gradient supplies local information. It does not reveal the complete path or guarantee that every landscape has one reachable bottom.

Gradient

The gradient is the direction and rate of steepest increase. In one dimension it is simply the derivative. Moving in its negative direction takes us downhill.

Learning rate

The learning rate α scales each update. Too small can be slow; too large can overshoot or diverge.

Convergence

Convergence means the parameters and objective stop changing appreciably. A common test is a very small gradient magnitude.

Learning-rate laboratory

For this particular quadratic, the distance from the minimum is multiplied by 1 − 2α after every step. That makes it unusually easy to see different behaviours.

Derive the slope—and the learning-rate behaviour

The lab reuses f(x)=x²+4x. We first derive its slope, then use algebra to explain where the factor 1−2α comes from.

1 · Differentiate each term. The power rule gives d(x²)/dx=2x, and d(4x)/dx=4.
f′(x)=2x+4=2(x+2)
2 · Put the slope into GD.
xₖ₊₁=xₖ−α(2xₖ+4)=xₖ−2α(xₖ+2)
3 · Measure distance from the minimum. Since x*=−2, the signed distance from it is xₖ−x*=xₖ+2.
xₖ₊₁+2 = xₖ+2−2α(xₖ+2)
4 · Factor the distance. This directly explains slow movement, oscillation, or divergence as α changes.
xₖ₊₁+2 = (1−2α)(xₖ+2)

A flat slope needs interpretation

A gradient of zero only says that the surface is locally flat. The surrounding shape tells us whether the point is a minimum, maximum, saddle point, or part of a flat region.

No universal guarantee: gradient descent can overshoot, diverge, settle at an undesirable stationary point, or stop early. Its result depends on the objective, starting values, learning rate, numerical precision, and stopping rule.

How does an iterative algorithm decide to stop?

A practical implementation declares “close enough” rather than waiting for perfect equality. Common stopping checks are:

Small gradient|f′(xₖ)| < ε: the local slope is tiny.
Small movement|xₖ₊₁−xₖ| < ε: the parameter barely changes.
Small improvement|f(xₖ₊₁)−f(xₖ)| < ε: the score barely improves.

maximum steps

Why not always solve f′(x)=0 directly?

For this small quadratic, solving 2x+4=0 immediately gives x=−2. Large models may have millions of coupled parameters and no convenient closed-form solution, so repeated numerical updates are practical.

Why use an iterative method?

For a small quadratic, setting the derivative to zero is quicker. Real models can contain thousands, millions, or billions of parameters, and their loss may have no practical one-shot algebraic formula—often called a closed-form solution. Gradient descent improves all parameters through repeated local corrections.

Where is it used?

Gradient-based optimization is used to fit prediction lines and curves, estimate class probabilities, train large neural networks, and tune many other systems whose adjustable parameters affect a numerical objective.

Batch GD uses the entire training set per update. Stochastic GD uses one example. Mini-batch GD uses a small group and is common in deep learning.

What exactly is a learning error?

A model learns by comparing its prediction with the known answer, converting that difference into a cost, and adjusting its parameters to reduce the cost.

Begin with a teacher’s penalty rule

A student predicts that an answer is 15, but the known answer is 12. The prediction is three units too high. The signed difference tells us both size and direction:

error = prediction − known answer = 15 − 12 = +3

The error describes the miss. A loss function is the teacher’s penalty rule: it decides how many penalty points that miss deserves. Different rules express different attitudes toward mistakes.

Loss functions give gradients something to solve

A loss function turns prediction quality into a smooth—or almost smooth—surface. Its slope tells gradient descent how the model should change.

A loss function is not a physical object that was “lost.” It is a deliberately chosen penalty rule. Its shape controls which mistakes receive strong corrections and therefore changes what gradient descent learns.

Regression losses

MSE = (1/m) Σᵢ₌₁ᵐ (ŷᵢ − yᵢ)²
Before sigma shorthand: for three examples, MSE=[(ŷ₁−y₁)²+(ŷ₂−y₂)²+(ŷ₃−y₃)²]/3. The symbol Σ only abbreviates this repeated addition.

MSE is mean squared error. Squaring prevents positive and negative residuals from cancelling and strongly penalizes large misses.

MAE = (1/m) Σᵢ₌₁ᵐ |ŷᵢ − yᵢ|

MAE is mean absolute error. It is less sensitive to outliers but uses a subgradient at residual zero—a permitted replacement slope chosen at a sharp corner where no unique derivative exists.

Where does the gradient come from?

w → ŷ=wx → r=ŷ−y → L=½r²

The chain rule follows the influence of w through every intermediate quantity:

dL/dw = (dL/dr)(dr/dŷ)(dŷ/dw)
dL/dw = r · 1 · x = (−3)(1)(2) = −6

What does the negative gradient mean?

dL/dw=−6 means increasing w a little will decrease the loss. The update subtracts the negative number:

w₁ = 1 − 0.1(−6) = 1.6

The new prediction is 1.6·2=3.2. Its residual is −1.8, so the new loss is ½(−1.8)²=1.62. The loss fell from 4.5 to 1.62.

Reconstruct the complete update yourself. Enter the six values in causal order.

Hint and solution

Hint and solution

f′(x)=2x−6, so f′(0)=−6. Then x₁=0−0.2(−6)=1.2.

3. Calculate MSE

Known targets are [2,4]; predictions are [3,2]. What is the MSE?

Hint and solution

Residuals are [1,−2]. Square them to get [1,4], then average: (1+4)/2=2.5.

4. Diagnose the learning rate

On f(x)=x²+4x, which value from the laboratory diverges?

Hint and solution

The distance from the minimum is multiplied by 1−2α. For α=1.10, the magnitude is |−1.2|>1, so the distance grows.

5. Choose a loss

You need regression that behaves like squared error for small residuals but is less affected by large outliers. Which loss fits?

Hint and solution

Huber loss is quadratic near zero and linear for large residuals, combining smooth small-error behaviour with outlier resistance.

6. Compute a weight gradient

For ŷ=wx, data x=[1,2], y=[2,4], and w=1, find ∂J/∂w using half-MSE.

Hint and solution

Predictions are [1,2], so residuals are [−1,−2]. The gradient is ((−1)(1)+(−2)(2))/2 = −2.5.

7. Identify the parameter

A bakery changes baking temperature to minimize a quality penalty. Which quantity is the parameter?

Hint and solution

The parameter is the controllable number the algorithm changes, so it is baking temperature. The penalty is the objective.

8. Predict the update direction

If the current derivative is −7 and α>0, does GD increase or decrease the parameter?

Hint and solution

x_new=x_old−α(−7)=x_old+7α, so the parameter increases.

9. Error or loss?

A prediction is 8 and the target is 10. The value −2 is the signed difference. What is it?

Hint and solution

The signed difference ŷ−y is the residual. A loss function would turn it into a penalty such as (−2)²=4.

10. Diagnose zero gradient

A calculation produces gradient zero. What can we safely conclude?

Hint and solution

Zero gradient establishes local flatness only. We need information about the surrounding landscape to classify the point.

Three complete model applications

The same update loop trains different models

The prediction formula and loss change, but the learning pattern remains: predict, measure error, differentiate, update, repeat.

Learned parameters versus chosen settings

Parameters such as weights and intercepts are changed by GD. Hyperparameters such as learning rate, mini-batch size, stopping tolerance, and polynomial degree are chosen outside the update rule.

What “generalize” means

A useful model should perform well on new examples, not merely remember its training rows. Training loss drives updates; validation performance guides model and hyperparameter choices; test data is reserved for final evaluation.

Feature scale can distort the journey

A house area might be around 1,500 while bedroom count is around 3. Because gradients multiply residuals by feature values, the area gradient can be numerically much larger even before we decide that area is more important.

Derive every partial derivative step by step

A partial derivative changes one parameter while temporarily holding the other parameters fixed. Define the residual for house i as rᵢ=ŷᵢ−yᵢ, and use squared-error cost J=(1/2m)Σrᵢ².

1 · Differentiate one squared residual. The chain rule supplies the derivative of the inside.
∂J/∂wⱼ = (1/2m) Σ 2rᵢ(∂rᵢ/∂wⱼ)
2 · Differentiate the residual. Since rᵢ=b+w₁xᵢ₁+w₂xᵢ₂−yᵢ, only the term containing wⱼ changes.
∂rᵢ/∂wⱼ = xᵢⱼ
3 · Substitute and cancel 2.
∂J/∂wⱼ = (1/m) Σ (ŷᵢ−yᵢ)xᵢⱼ
4 · Derive the intercept separately. Since ∂rᵢ/∂b=1:
∂J/∂b = (1/2m) Σ 2rᵢ·1 = (1/m) Σ (ŷᵢ−yᵢ)

wⱼ ← wⱼ−α(∂J/∂wⱼ),   b ← b−α(∂J/∂b)

Plain-language glossary

Parameter
A number the learning algorithm is allowed to change, such as a weight.
Objective
A numerical score used to compare possible parameter choices.
Model
A rule that turns observed inputs into a prediction using parameters.
Prediction
The model’s current proposed answer.
Residual/error
The signed difference between prediction and known target.
Loss
A penalty assigned to one prediction mistake.
Cost
A combined loss over training examples; often the objective minimized in ML.
Derivative
How quickly one output changes locally as one input changes.
Gradient
The collection of partial derivatives for every adjustable parameter; it points toward steepest local increase.
Learning rate
The multiplier controlling how much of the gradient becomes one update.
Iteration
One pass through calculate-gradient, update-parameters, and re-evaluate.
Convergence
The process settling enough to satisfy a stated stopping rule.
Generalization
Performing well on relevant examples not used to calculate the updates.
Hyperparameter
A training or model setting selected outside GD, such as learning rate or polynomial degree.

Final end-to-end explanation

Choose parameters
Make outcomes or predictions
Calculate objective or loss
Find the gradient
Update and verify

Repeat until an appropriate stopping condition is met. A low numerical objective is evidence that the supplied score improved—not automatic proof that the real-world system is useful, safe, fair, or complete.

Use these six questions everywhere: What are we changing? What score are we minimizing? How does the gradient choose direction? How far do we move? Did the score improve? Why did we stop?
